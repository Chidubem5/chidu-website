About Me

Hey, I'm Chidubem! My name comes from the Igbo language and translates to "God Guide Me" or "I will follow in God's footsteps". It's something I genuinely live by. I hold dual US and Nigerian citizenship, and that blend of cultures has given me a perspective I wouldn't trade for anything.

I studied Physics at Bates College, picking up minors in Computer Science and Mathematics along the way. Right now I'm getting my Master's in Physics at the University of Central Florida, where I work in Dr. Enrique Del Barco's lab on adiabatic spintronics. In plain terms, I'm figuring out how spin currents move through antiferromagnetic materials. I'm also part of NSBP and the Graduate Society of Physics Students, which keeps me connected to a great community of scientists and engineers.

Outside the lab, I love digging into problems from every angle. I went through the Erdős Institute Quant Finance Boot Camp and earned a Google Analytics certification because honestly, I just find patterns fascinating wherever they show up. Financial markets, social dynamics, data pipelines... if there's something to figure out, I'm probably already thinking about it.

I also competed as a collegiate athlete at Bates, playing football as a Defensive End and Outside Linebacker and running track in sprints, high jump, long jump, and shot put. And I've had some incredible travel experiences too. My trips to India and South Africa were service trips through Noble and Greenough School where I got to volunteer in education and community health. Those trips really stuck with me.

Conference Poster 2024

Spin Ground State and Optical Excitations in the Vacancy/Oxygen Defect in the Hexagonal BN

Umeh, C., Alcántara Ortigoza, M., Stolbov, S.

National Society of Black Physicists (NSBP) Annual Conference  ·  Houston, TX  ·  Poster PDF

For a General Audience

Quantum computers promise to solve problems that are completely out of reach for today's machines, but they require a fundamentally different kind of information storage: the qubit. Unlike a classical bit, which is either 0 or 1, a qubit exploits quantum mechanics to exist in both states simultaneously. One of the most exciting ways to build qubits is to engineer them into atomic-scale defects inside solid materials — microscopic imperfections in an otherwise perfect crystal where a small cluster of atoms behaves in a controllable quantum way.

This poster investigates a specific defect in hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), a two-dimensional material prized for its large energy gap and its ability to host optically active defects. The defect, called VBON, is formed by removing a boron atom and replacing a neighboring nitrogen atom with oxygen. Prior work predicted this arrangement would produce a magnetically useful "triplet" spin state — a configuration considered ideal for spin-qubit applications.

Our calculations tell a different story. While a triplet state does exist, it turns out to be dynamically unstable: the crystal would spontaneously distort away from it. Following that distortion leads to a more stable "singlet" state that sits 0.24 eV lower in energy. Although this rules out VBON as a spin qubit, it opens a different door — the singlet state produces a sharp, bright optical excitation at 2.5 eV (visible light), making this defect a promising candidate for a single-photon emitter, a key component in quantum communication and cryptography.

For a Technically Familiar Audience

We characterize the VBON defect in 2D hexagonal boron nitride using a hierarchy of first-principles methods: DFT for structural relaxation and total energies, GW for quasiparticle corrections, and the Bethe-Salpeter equation (BSE) for the optical excitation spectrum. The supercell is a 5×5×1 hBN sheet; the defect comprises a B-vacancy with an adjacent O-substituted N-atom, yielding two dangling bonds and an even valence electron count.

DFT confirms a triplet ground state consistent with prior reports, but phonon analysis reveals imaginary frequency modes in the triplet VBON — a clear signature of dynamic instability. Displacing the structure along these imaginary phonon eigenvectors drives a transition to a singlet state that is 0.24 eV lower in total energy and exhibits no imaginary modes, confirming its dynamic stability. The singlet VBON formation energy is 2.33 eV under both N-rich and N-poor conditions, far below the analogous VBCN defect (9.234 eV), indicating thermodynamically favorable formation in oxygen-containing growth environments.

GW calculations reveal local in-gap states for the singlet configuration. BSE then yields a sharp excitonic resonance at hν = 2.5 eV in Im(ε), with large oscillator strength in the in-plane (xx and yy) polarization channels; the zz contribution is negligible. This prominent, narrow optical feature positions VBON as a strong single-photon emitter candidate in the visible range, even as the singlet ground state disqualifies it from spin-qubit applications requiring a non-zero spin.

Senior Thesis 2022

Exploring the Relationship between Stellar Mass, Star Formation Rate, and Gas Outflow Velocity in a Large Sample of Galaxies

Umeh, C., et al.

Unpublished  ·  Bates College Senior Thesis  ·  PDF

For a General Audience

Galaxies like our Milky Way are surprisingly bad at making stars. Even though galaxies contain enormous amounts of gas — the raw material for stars — only a tiny fraction of it ever becomes one. Scientists believe a major culprit is galactic outflows: powerful winds of gas that get ejected from a galaxy, taking potential star-forming material with them and slowing the whole process down.

This thesis investigates those outflows by studying a sample of 300,000 galaxies drawn from a massive sky survey called eBOSS. The goal is to understand how a galaxy's size (measured by stellar mass) and its rate of star formation connect to how fast gas is being blown out of it.

To measure star formation, I rely on a specific wavelength of light called H-alpha, which is emitted when hydrogen atoms get energized by newborn stars and then release that energy as light. By measuring how bright this light is, one can calculate how many stars a galaxy is currently producing. A complication is that cosmic dust absorbs some of this light — particularly at shorter wavelengths — making galaxies look dimmer than they really are. I developed careful mathematical corrections to account for this dust and recover the true star formation rates.

The main finding is that the galaxies forming stars most rapidly are also the dustiest, which means the dust correction is especially important for the most active galaxies. This work lays the groundwork for follow-up research that will stack thousands of galaxy spectra together to directly measure how fast gas is being expelled, and whether faster star formation leads to faster outflows.

Completing this research took more than an understanding of space — it took code. I built the Python pipeline that processed and analyzed data from hundreds of thousands of galaxies: reading large datasets, applying mathematical corrections for dust, calculating luminosities, and generating every plot and figure in the thesis. On the astronomy side, it meant understanding how light interacts with matter at the atomic level, how to read a spectrum as a galaxy's chemical and kinematic fingerprint, and how to account for redshift — the stretching of light from distant galaxies caused by the universe's expansion. Tying it together meant translating physical theory into working code, then critically checking whether the results actually made physical sense.

For a Technically Familiar Audience

This thesis establishes the observational and computational pipeline for characterizing stellar mass, star formation rate (SFR), and gas outflow velocity across a sample of ~300,000 star-forming galaxies from the eBOSS survey (0.4 < z < 1.2), representing roughly 10% of the total eBOSS target catalog.

The core methodology centers on dust-corrected hydrogen recombination line luminosities as SFR tracers. For galaxies at z < 0.58, where H-alpha falls within the spectrograph's wavelength range (360–1000 nm, R ~ 2000), the Balmer decrement (H-alpha/H-beta) is used to compute color excess E(B-V) following the Momcheva et al. (2013) attenuation framework. With k(H-alpha) = 3.33 and k(H-beta) = 4.60, dust correction factors reaching ~100x are applied to recover intrinsic H-alpha flux. SFR is then derived via the Kennicutt & Evans (2012) calibration: log(SFR) = log(L_Hα) − 41.27

For galaxies at z > 0.58, where H-alpha redshifts out of the detector range, the H-beta/H-gamma ratio serves as the Balmer decrement proxy, with corrected H-beta luminosity scaled by 2.86 to approximate intrinsic H-alpha luminosity. A consistent result across both redshift regimes is the positive correlation between emission-line luminosity and dust attenuation — higher SFR galaxies exhibit systematically larger Balmer decrements, consistent with the picture that the most intensely star-forming systems are embedded in dustier ISM environments.

This work is explicitly framed as the SFR characterization stage of a larger project. The subsequent phase will perform spectral stacking in bins of SFR and stellar mass to measure outflow velocities from blueshifted absorption features — enabling direct empirical tests of competing outflow scaling relations (e.g., velocity scaling with stellar mass per Oppenheimer & Dave 2006, vs. star formation surface density per Heckman et al. 2015).

I built this pipeline entirely in Python, using Astropy to handle the FITS-format spectroscopic data. That meant implementing flux extraction, signal-to-noise filtering (SNR > 3 on both H-alpha and H-beta), Balmer decrement calculations, attenuation curve evaluations, and luminosity distance conversions across the full 300,000-object sample. On the astrophysics side, it demanded fluency in nebular emission physics, ISM dust attenuation modeling, cosmological distance and lookback-time calculations under the FLRW framework, and the observational constraints imposed by fixed spectrograph wavelength coverage — specifically, knowing when and why to switch Balmer tracers as a function of redshift.

Journal Article 2021

Compact Starburst Galaxies with Fast Outflows: Central Escape Velocities and Stellar Mass Surface Densities from Multi-band Hubble Space Telescope Imaging

Diamond-Stanic et al. (incl. C. Umeh)

Astrophysical Journal, 912, 11  ·  DOI ↗

For a General Audience

When we think about galaxies, we tend to imagine slow, majestic systems evolving over billions of years. But some galaxies are caught in an extreme and brief phase of activity — forming stars at a furious rate while simultaneously blasting gas outward at extraordinary speeds. This paper focuses on 12 of these rare, compact starburst galaxies, observed using the Hubble Space Telescope, located billions of light years away.

What makes these galaxies remarkable is the sheer violence of their outflows. Gas is being ejected at speeds between 1,000 and 3,000 kilometers per second — fast enough that it can escape the galaxy entirely and travel into the vast space surrounding it. To put that in perspective, that is roughly 0.3% to 1% the speed of light. The central regions of these galaxies are also extraordinarily dense and compact, with half their light coming from a region only about 100 light years across — tiny by galactic standards.

The central question the paper tries to answer is: what is actually launching these outflows? To investigate this, the authors measured the escape velocity at the center of each galaxy — essentially, how fast something would need to move to break free of the galaxy's gravitational pull. Strikingly, the observed outflow speeds are about twice as fast as the central escape velocity. This tells us that whatever is driving these winds is extraordinarily powerful, powerful enough to not only eject gas from the galaxy's core but to send it far out into the surrounding cosmic environment, potentially never to return.

The paper also finds that the density of stars packed into these galactic cores is near a theoretical maximum known as the Eddington limit — the point at which radiation pressure from stars can push outward as strongly as gravity pulls inward. This gives a strong hint that radiation pressure from the intense burst of star formation may be one of the key engines driving these violent outflows.

The authors speculate that these galaxies represent a dramatic transitional moment in galactic evolution, and that they may eventually settle into a particular class of older, quieter elliptical galaxies seen in the present-day universe. This research was made possible through Hubble Space Telescope imaging across multiple wavelengths of light, from near-ultraviolet to near-infrared, allowing the team to disentangle the properties of the young starburst component from the older underlying galaxy. I contributed by writing Python code to process the imaging data and producing the scientific visualizations that helped communicate and interpret the results.

For a Technically Familiar Audience

This paper presents multiband Hubble Space Telescope imaging spanning rest-frame near-UV through near-IR (λ_rest = 0.3–1.1 μm) for 12 compact starburst galaxies at z = 0.4–0.8 with stellar masses M* ~ 1011 solar masses. These systems are driving some of the most extreme galactic outflows known, with maximum outflow velocities v_max = 1,000–3,000 km/s, and their light profiles are dominated by an ultra-compact starburst component with half-light radii of only ~100 pc.

The central scientific objective is to constrain the physical mechanisms responsible for launching these fast outflows by characterizing conditions within the central kiloparsec. Stellar population synthesis modeling reveals that this compact central component contributes roughly 25% of the total stellar mass despite its small physical extent. Critically, the derived central escape velocities v_esc,central ~ 900 km/s are a factor of ~2 below the observed outflow velocities — a result that places strong constraints on the driving mechanism, requiring a physical process capable of accelerating gas well beyond what gravitational potential alone would suggest as a ceiling. This firmly establishes that a significant fraction of the outflowing material has sufficient kinetic energy to reach the circumgalactic medium and potentially achieve true escape from the halo entirely.

The measured central stellar mass surface densities Σ_e,central ~ 3×1011 solar masses per kpc2 are comparable to theoretical Eddington limit estimates, raising the possibility that radiation pressure on dust is a significant contributor to the outflow driving. The Σ_1 surface densities within the central kiloparsec are consistent with those of compact massive galaxies across 0.5 < z < 3.0. Compared to both red nuggets and blue nuggets at z ~ 2, these galaxies show systematically smaller effective radii at fixed stellar mass, which the authors attribute to the photometric dominance of the young starburst component and the improved physical resolution afforded by observing rest-frame optical emission at z ~ 0.6 rather than z ~ 2.

The authors compare these systems against theoretical formation scenarios including major mergers and violent disk instability, and speculate that these compact starbursts are progenitors of local power-law ellipticals with prominent stellar cusps — placing them at a pivotal evolutionary juncture between gas-rich, star-forming systems and quiescent early-type galaxies. The analysis pipeline underlying these results was built in Python, with contributions including data reduction, stellar population fitting, surface brightness profile decomposition, and the generation of scientific visualizations using standard astronomical Python packages such as NumPy, Matplotlib, and Astropy.

Conference Abstract 2020

Compact Starburst Galaxies with Fast Outflows: Constraints on Recent Star Formation and Outflow Properties from Optical Spectroscopy and Multi-Wavelength Photometry

Bradna et al. (incl. C. Umeh)

American Astronomical Society Meeting #235, ID 111.05  ·  Bulletin of the American Astronomical Society, Vol. 52, No. 1  ·  ADS ↗

For a General Audience

One of the biggest unsolved mysteries in astronomy is understanding how massive galaxies stop forming stars. Most large galaxies we see today are essentially "dead" — they stopped producing new stars billions of years ago and have remained quiet ever since. But how did they get there? Something had to shut the process down, and this paper investigates one of the most compelling suspects: violent outflows of gas driven by intense bursts of star formation.

The paper focuses on a sample of about a dozen massive, compact galaxies that are caught in what appears to be a pivotal transitional moment. These galaxies were recently forming stars at an extraordinary rate, but their current optical signatures suggest that star formation has already begun to fade. At the same time, they are hurling gas outward at speeds exceeding 1,000 kilometers per second — fast enough to strip a galaxy of the very fuel it needs to keep making stars.

To study these galaxies, the team used data from two major observatories — the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the Keck Telescope — alongside observations spanning from ultraviolet to infrared light. By examining the way certain wavelengths of light are absorbed by outflowing gas, the researchers were able to trace the structure and speed of these winds. They found that the outflowing gas appears to form a shell-like structure that covers a large fraction of the galaxy, consistent with gas being swept up and expelled in a powerful wave.

The central conclusion is that there is a tight connection between the shutting down of star formation and the expulsion of gas reserves through these outflows. In other words, the outflows are not just a side effect of intense star formation — they may be actively causing it to stop by removing the raw material needed to make new stars. The paper also examines which physical processes — including supernova explosions, stellar winds, radiation pressure, cosmic rays, and black hole activity — are capable of accelerating gas to the observed speeds, placing meaningful constraints on each.

This research was made possible through a combination of spectroscopic analysis and multi-wavelength photometric modeling, requiring contributors to work across a broad range of data types and physical scales. My role involved Python-based data analysis and building the scientific visualizations — translating complex multi-wavelength datasets into interpretable figures that supported the team's broader conclusions.

For a Technically Familiar Audience

This paper addresses the poorly understood transition of massive galaxies from actively star-forming to quiescent, using a sample of ~12 compact starburst galaxies known to be driving large-scale outflows with v > 1,000 km/s. The dataset combines optical spectroscopy from SDSS and Keck with UV-through-IR photometry, enabling a joint analysis of emission-line diagnostics and spectral energy distribution (SED) modeling.

SED fitting reveals that these galaxies hosted extraordinarily high star formation rates in the recent past, yet their current optical emission lines are surprisingly weak — a signature consistent with rapid quenching of the recent starburst. This combination places these systems in a brief and rarely observed post-starburst transitional phase, making them valuable laboratories for studying feedback-driven quenching in real time.

Outflow properties are characterized through interstellar absorption-line profiles, which provide kinematic information on the cool gas phase of the wind. The analysis reveals large covering fractions that vary systematically as a function of velocity, a signature consistent with a swept-up shell model of outflowing interstellar gas rather than a smooth, volume-filling wind. This geometric and kinematic structure has direct implications for estimating mass outflow rates and the efficiency with which these winds can deplete the interstellar gas reservoir.

The absorption-line profiles are further used to constrain the physical driving mechanisms — supernovae, stellar winds, radiation pressure, cosmic rays, and AGN accretion — by comparing observed outflow velocities and momentum fluxes against the predictions of each scenario. The close correspondence between the quenching of the starburst and the observed gas expulsion strongly implicates feedback-driven outflows as a direct causal agent in star formation suppression, rather than a coincidental byproduct. This positions the sample as observational evidence for the kind of rapid, feedback-induced quenching invoked in theoretical models of massive galaxy evolution.

The analysis pipeline was implemented in Python, with contributions including multi-wavelength photometric data handling, SED fitting support, absorption-line profile analysis, and the generation of publication-quality visualizations using standard scientific Python packages including NumPy, Matplotlib, and Astropy.
